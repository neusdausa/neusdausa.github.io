When Expectations (Don't) Move Together: Inflation, Growth, and Household Consumption

Using ECB Consumer Expectations Survey data for eleven euro area countries (2020–2025), I classify households by whether their inflation and growth expectations move together ("demand view") or oppositely ("supply view") In contrast to previous findings, at least a third of households hold a demand view, varying widely across countries and by age, gender, and expected personal finances. These views persist strongly over time, suggesting stable mental models, and predict meaningful differences in consumption of total spending, necessities, and durables. Trust in the ECB raises the likelihood of a demand view, linking central bank credibility to consumption behavior. I am currently embedding these findings into a New Keynesian model.

Labor Market Experiences, Expectations, and Behavior With Diego Vila

This paper shows that personal experiences in the labor market influence workers' expectations about the evolution of macroeconomic variables. Using FEDNY SCE data we document that individuals who recently lost their job become more pessimistic and uncertain. We propose a new channel through which variation in labor market status could have further implication on households' consumption patterns. Becoming unemployed has a negative impact on personal consumption through a direct reduction of income and an indirect effect of worsened future economic prospects which triggers a more prudent behavior and increases the precautionary motive. We use a memory based model to show how personal experiences distort beliefs.

The Asymmetric Adjustment of Global Imbalances: Myth or Fact?, with Livio Stracca
ECB Working Paper No. 2777

This paper studies the long-standing claim that current account imbalances are corrected mainly by deficit countries, with surplus countries facing little pressure to adjust. It develops a simple model showing that policymakers who account for a debt-related externality have an incentive to tighten fiscal policy more aggressively when facing a deficit than when facing a surplus. Using data on large current account adjustment episodes across advanced and emerging economies, the paper finds only limited evidence of asymmetry in broad macroeconomic patterns. However, when isolating current account movements driven by exogenous oil price shocks, fiscal policy is found to tighten significantly only in response to shocks that push countries toward deficit, and this effect is stronger for countries experiencing banking crises. The findings suggest the "secular international problem" is real but more nuanced than commonly assumed.
